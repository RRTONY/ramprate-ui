# The Cloud Gets Rich. What Do Its Neighbors Get?

A challenge to data center developers: put community prosperity, real decision rights, and your reputation inside the deal.

By Tony Greenberg

A data center can serve the world and still disappoint the people across the street.

That is the deal I want to change.

I entered this industry in March 1996. I never left. We have continued working in it, and some interesting projects are now taking us deeper. Through ImpactSoul, I want to help connect the wealth compute creates to the wellbeing of the people who make it possible.

We are remarkably good at finding room for another rack. Surely we can find room in the agreement for a neighbor.

At YOTTA, I spent time with people whose kindness I have known for decades. That experience is the subject of the companion essay. This one asks what happens when kindness has to survive a refinancing, a leadership change, or a sale.

The proposals here are mine. Dean Nelson, Peter Gross, George Rockett, and iMasons are not being presented as endorsers of ImpactSoul or this model.

## Communities help create asset value

A project needs land, power, infrastructure, workers, and people willing to live alongside it. Those contributions make development possible.

If a community matters when we calculate an asset’s value, why should it become peripheral when we distribute that value?

My conviction is that communities that prosper can help projects prosper. That relationship needs evidence. We should measure affordability, employment, health, access, trust, and operational results rather than assume every charitable dollar produces a valuation premium.

Community wellbeing deserves to be a design requirement. It also deserves honest arithmetic.

## Publish the bargain before the ribbon cutting

You are asking people to live beside your ambition. Let them prosper beside it.

A developer arrives with investors, engineers, and a forecast. The neighbors arrive with lives already underway. Children. Water bills. A place they intend to grow old.

Before asking for approval, explain who pays for power, water, roads, and shared infrastructure. Distinguish construction jobs from permanent employment. Name the people eligible for training and the employers expected to hire them.

Put a budget, a responsible party, a delivery date, and a remedy beside each commitment. Make the obligations survive changes in ownership where the legal structure permits and the agreement requires it.

A promise should not get to retire before the community does.

Developers need this clarity, too. Named obligations can be priced. Unnamed obligations eventually become surprises. Surprises are delightful at birthday parties and less delightful in infrastructure finance.

## Give people authority they can use

Decentralized governance must distribute actual power.

Who chooses the community representatives? Which decisions do they control? Can they obtain independent advice? Can residents challenge or replace them? What happens when the operator ignores an obligation?

Publish those answers.

Otherwise, we have built a suggestion box with a wallet attached.

For Indigenous and Tribal communities, start with sovereignty. Consent, control over data, and the right to refuse belong at the beginning of the conversation. A token does not manufacture consent. A charity check does not purchase it.

The Hoopa Valley Tribe offers a useful example at its own scale. Its Acorn network and data center serve a different purpose from a hyperscale AI campus. Published reporting describes Tribal operation, household connections, and a continuing expansion program.[1]

The lesson I take is about control: people who need a service can help govern the infrastructure delivering it. The results still need scrutiny. Who is connected? At what cost? With what reliability? Who remains outside?

Local authority should come with the ability to inspect the answer.

## Make the money visible

Publish the distribution formula, fees, decision rights, and payments. If you promise affordable compute, show the capacity, eligibility, and price.

Protect personal and culturally sensitive information. Make the project economics understandable.

Follow the dollars all the way through: project earns cash; contractual obligations are paid; the agreed community share is calculated; authorized representatives decide its use; funded programs report what they delivered.

A training budget is an input. A completed course is an output. A lasting job is an outcome. Put them in separate columns. The unemployed graduate does not need another photograph of the check presentation.

Through ImpactSoul, I want to connect this discipline to a regenerative tomorrow across compute, medicine, and other areas. A clinic with better access. A researcher with affordable capacity. A family with more security. Define the benefit, fund it, and check whether it happened.

## A share of the future, with real rights inside it

Shared ownership is one possible mechanism. My proposed community target is participation in one-percent increments toward ten percent. A broader project float could reach thirty percent in some structures; that is a separate possibility, not the community target or a forecast.

Each increment needs an explicit source of funding, price, allocation rule, and set of rights. A community discount is a concession borne by someone. It is not costless generosity.

Giving residents a funded benefit is different from asking them to invest their savings. The second introduces investment risk. Keep those promises separate.

Tokenization may represent equity, debt, revenue rights, or access to compute. Those are different claims. The legal documents and operating structure determine what someone actually owns.

A token is a container. You can put an enforceable right inside it. You can also put a very expensive wish.

Who gets paid first? Who absorbs losses? What happens if the operator fails? What can a holder sell, to whom, and under which restrictions?

A secondary market requires willing buyers and a functioning, permitted trading arrangement. A target return is an assumption to test. Nobody gets to promise liquidity because the spreadsheet is wearing violet.

I want community influence designed to endure even when individual holdings change. That requires governance documents and enforceable agreements. It will not happen automatically because we say “forever” with conviction.

The financial companion shows an illustrative $1 billion project, ownership allocations, cash flows, discounts, and assumptions. It is a model to examine, not an investment offer or a guaranteed outcome.[2]

## Put your reputation beside your promise

The developer. The investor. The operator. Me, when I bring people into the room.

Keep our names attached to what we commit to deliver. Build an independently checked record of commitments, performance, failures, and repair. Let communities contribute evidence and contest the record. Give the people responsible a fair opportunity to respond and correct mistakes.

That is the reputation economy I want to build: verified conduct shaping who earns confidence, capital, and the opportunity to build again.

A team that repairs a failure deserves credit for the repair. The original failure stays visible.

Otherwise, a promise can enjoy a magnificent launch and a remarkably private funeral. The ribbon gets photographed. The missing benefit gets referred to another department.

## More joy belongs in the model

I have spent enough time around money to know it can be excellent at accumulating and surprisingly vague about its destination.

Families need security. People need useful work, education, care, and room to enjoy being alive. A developer can earn a return while helping create those conditions. The question is whether the bargain actually does it.

I came looking for a beneficial universe. Naturally, I ended up asking who had read the contract.

Conscious compute should mean that the people making growth possible can influence the bargain and see a meaningful share of its benefits. That is the standard I want to help develop through ImpactSoul.

Developers: tell us about your project. Let’s examine the economics, the community’s priorities, and what meaningful participation could look like. Bring the financing structure, the difficult questions, and the promises you are prepared to put your name behind.

The cloud has neighbors. Build a deal you would be proud to explain at their kitchen table.
