# Data Centers Have a Heart. Meet the Humans Behind the Cloud.

Behind the infrastructure are people worth knowing. Three days at YOTTA reminded me why their kindness belongs in the story, and why the neighbors deserve to feel it.

By Tony Greenberg

The future has a power cord. The neighbors would like to know who gets plugged in and who gets the invoice.

For three days at YOTTA in Las Vegas, I listened as we tried to fit tomorrow into a building and find enough electricity to wake it up. Chips. Capital. Power. Compute beyond Earth. Apparently, one planet was becoming an inconvenient starter home.

I also went to see old friends. People who remember earlier versions of me and still answer the phone. An underrated form of renewable energy.

Peter Gross. George Rockett. Dean Nelson.

The people behind this machinery are some of the kindest humans I know. That does not settle a dispute over electricity, water, or land. It does complicate the cartoon in which everyone building a data center is a villain with a cooling tower.

I have watched these people build schools, gather extraordinary minds, and give their time to work far beyond their companies. Their kindness deserves to be seen. The industry’s obligations deserve to be examined. We can manage both thoughts without overheating.

## Only time buys trust

I entered this business in March 1996. I pushed for CO-LOCATION, with the hyphen. Two things sharing a place. Even the spelling had to learn to live together.

By September 1996, I remember us using both “Internet Data Center” and “CO-LOCATION” in printed advertising. I worked to establish our identity as the Internet Data Center company, following conversations with Barry James Folsom, and later to document that history on Wikipedia.* The industry was finding its language. We were finding our way.

I founded RampRate in 2000 to bring transparency and better decisions to technology deals. I have spent years crawling through the plumbing between a promise and a contract. Glamorous work. Occasionally there is a spreadsheet.

A cheap price can be a magic trick in which the cost disappears from your bill and reappears in somebody else’s life.

I never left this industry. We have kept working in it all along. Some interesting projects are now pulling us deeper. Through ImpactSoul, I want to connect the wealth compute creates to better health, education, opportunity, and a meaningful say for the communities that make it possible.

At YOTTA, I spent time with friends whose work deserves recognition in its own right.

## Peter: keeping the machinery alive, remembering why

Peter Gross is one of my dearest friends. The industry’s great uncle and godfather, in my affections.

His career includes co-founding EYP Mission Critical Facilities and leading PMG Associates. His service on UCLA’s Institute of the Environment and Sustainability advisory board and the Los Angeles Ballet board reaches beyond the infrastructure he helped shape.[1]

Keeping a server alive is an accomplishment. Remembering what makes life worth keeping is another.

## George: a curator of humans

George Rockett built DCD and YOTTA as a founder. I watched him do it step by step, with courage, brilliance, intellect, persuasion, and a great conversation.

He gathers people from the industry and far beyond it, then gives their collective intelligence room to become something extraordinary. I crave our conversations. His knowledge runs deep. His curiosity travels widely. I rarely leave thinking exactly as I did when I arrived.

George is one of the great catalysts for what I call GEMSPARK: the fusion of serendipity and synchronicity. An unexpected encounter meets a meaningful alignment, and a possibility comes alive. That is my concept, not the name of his organization.

He can assemble a room where the conversation changes what becomes possible.

At YOTTA, George and I held a puppy in a hall devoted to tomorrow’s machinery. For a moment, the future had paws. The puppy had no position on grid interconnection and made no claims about transforming humanity. I found this refreshing.

## Dean: the conscience has a work history

Dean Nelson’s care has a history. A 2007 trip to India with his mother, Sandy, set them on the path to Just Let Me Learn. Schools, transport, tutoring: the infrastructure of a childhood.[2]

I funded the soccer field called the RampRate Field of Dreams at Sesawng.[3] A place to play is a place to belong. Geeks Giving Back rallied people around the school; iMasons raised $50,000 for it at its first meeting in 2016.[4]

Dean’s work through Cato Digital also matters to me. I am an investor in Cato. That relationship is separate from the proposals I am developing through ImpactSoul.

Infrastructure Masons deserves recognition for its own work. I see it as a social-impact layer and a conscience for the compute industry: a place where the people building the machinery can face the responsibilities that come with it.

Its Social Accord brings communities into the conversation. Its Climate Accord addresses emissions. Its Foundation invests in people through education and professional pathways.[5]

The Foundation’s published programs include capstone projects, training, DCD Academy credentials, and a $2,500 scholarship for each Capstone student. Its AI initiative describes a 250-student SMU hackathon and a goal of 1,000 students in its first 2026 cohort.[6] Those are concrete ways to open the industry to its next generation. Published targets tell us the ambition; delivered results tell us the impact.

Watching the iMasons Awards was better than the Academy Awards. I waited for a seat. The room felt more in demand than the highest-value chip in the world.

The prize was recognition. People who keep the invisible machine running. People trying to make its future worthy of the power it consumes. In a week of gargantuan forecasts, that room gave me a human-scale definition of value.

## The cloud has neighbors

Did you hear all the data centers in the world are shutting down?

Yep. They’re putting them in the cloud.

A magnificent disappearing act. The buildings vanish from the sentence while the land, water, and electricity remain stubbornly on Earth.

Follow the cloud home and you find a neighborhood. Someone would quite like to sleep.

One of the most interesting people I met at YOTTA was Tyler Norris. His co-authored Duke paper, Rethinking Load Growth, examines how briefly reducing demand during the grid’s busiest periods could make room for more customers.[7]

The local engineering still has to work. You cannot plug a building into a promising paper. But the question matters: can being a good neighbor include knowing when to ease off the grid? And if that flexibility creates savings, who receives them?

Even inside the largest companies, there are people working on questions worth taking seriously. Before casting an evil eye at an entire industry, look closely. Then ask the hard questions anyway.

## Bring the alignment down to Earth

Syzygy has been part of my foundation’s name for more than a decade. Celestial bodies coming into alignment. A word that looks as though the alphabet fell down the stairs and landed on an idea worth keeping.

I fell in love with it when I learned of its earlier trademark connection to my friend Nolan Bushnell. That is my recollection. There is a mezcalrita story. Another time.

At YOTTA, that old love acquired an earthly assignment: bring energy, intelligence, capital, and human dignity into the same sky. Then come down and make the agreement work.

This is my assessment of the industry and the good people I know behind it. Their kindness is real to me. Public concern deserves more than reassurance.

A generous person can change a life. An enforceable agreement can protect people that person will never meet, long after the project changes hands.

Through ImpactSoul, I want to explore how compute can help advance medicine, widen opportunity, and support a regenerative tomorrow. Those are my ambitions. Dean, Peter, George, and iMasons are being recognized for their own work, not presented as endorsers of ImpactSoul or my ownership proposals.

Good people helped build this industry. My challenge is to make their neighbors feel that goodness in the terms, the bills, and the benefits. Kindness should survive the ribbon cutting.
